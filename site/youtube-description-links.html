YouTube Description Links: A Strategy That Converts

Most creators treat the description as an afterthought. It is one of the few pieces of real estate on YouTube you fully control, and the single best place to turn a view into a click you can measure. Here is how to use it on purpose.

Your description is owned real estate

On YouTube you rent attention. The algorithm decides who sees your video, how long the thumbnail stays in rotation, and when a recommendation surfaces. The one thing you own outright is the description and the comment you pin underneath it. Nobody can bury those, reorder them, or charge you for them. That makes them the most reliable channel you have for sending a viewer somewhere that belongs to you, whether that is a landing page, a store, or a newsletter sign-up.

Owned real estate only matters if you use it with intent. A description stuffed with a wall of links, hashtags, and affiliate codes asks the viewer to make a decision you should have made for them. The goal is not to list everything you offer. The goal is to point one clear arrow at the next step and make it easy to follow.

Put one tracked link above the fold

YouTube collapses the description after a couple of lines. On most screens a viewer sees three lines before the "more" cutoff, and the overwhelming majority never tap to expand. So the link that matters has to live in those first lines, above the fold, before any timestamps or credits. If your call to action is sitting in paragraph four, it effectively does not exist.

Make that link a tracked link rather than a raw URL. A tracked link is a short, branded address that records every click with the source and campaign it came from. Instead of pasting a long landing page URL, you create something clean like a short Schedlytics link, drop it in the first line, and let it do the counting for you. If you are new to this, the link tracking overview walks through how a short link records clicks, unique visitors, and sources, and the free UTM builder helps you tag the link so it shows up cleanly in your analytics.

Attribute traffic to the exact video

This is where a tracked link earns its keep. When every video points to the same naked URL, your website analytics show a pile of "YouTube" traffic with no way to tell which upload sent it. A tracked link, or a unique campaign tag per video, breaks that pile apart. Now you can line up a specific video next to the clicks it produced and the signups that followed, the same way Schedlytics YouTube analytics sits views next to clicks and revenue.

Per video attribution changes how you plan. A tutorial with modest views that drives a high click rate is worth more to your business than a viral video that sends nobody anywhere. Without tracking you would chase the view count. With it you can see which formats actually move people, and you can track content that drives sales rather than content that merely gets watched.

Write a call to action that earns the click

A bare link is not a reason to click. The line around it is. Tell the viewer exactly what is on the other side and why it helps them right now. "Link in the description" is weak. "Get the free checklist from this video" or "Grab the template I used at minute four" gives a concrete payoff tied to what they just watched.

Reinforce it out loud. The strongest description links are the ones the host mentions in the video, because spoken intent is what sends a viewer hunting for the link in the first place. Match the words you say to the words next to the link, so the moment a viewer decides to act, the path is obvious. One clear ask, phrased as a benefit, beats five vague ones every time.

Use a pinned comment for Shorts

Shorts are a different surface. The description is mostly hidden and the link is hard to reach, so the description strategy falls apart. The fix is the pinned comment. Post your tracked link as the first comment the moment the Short goes live, pin it, and say "pinned comment" in the video or on screen so viewers know where to look.

A pinned comment also works as a second slot on long form videos. You can keep the primary link in the description above the fold and pin a comment with the same link or a related offer, which gives a second visible entry point without cluttering the description itself. Use the same tracked link in both places so the clicks roll up to one number per video.

Keep one primary link, not a wall

It is tempting to list your shop, your newsletter, your Discord, three affiliate codes, and your other social profiles. Every extra link you add lowers the odds that anyone follows the one that matters. Attention is finite, and a long menu asks the viewer to do work you should have done for them.

Pick the single most important destination for this video and make it the obvious choice. Secondary links can live further down for the small share of viewers who expand the description, but the top of the description should read like one clean instruction, not a directory. If you genuinely need to offer several destinations, send the primary link to a simple landing page and let that page hold the menu, so you still get one trackable click and a place to measure what people do next.

Review which videos drive clicks, then repeat the format

None of this pays off unless you close the loop. Once links are tracked per video, set a regular habit, monthly is plenty, of looking at which videos sent the most clicks and which of those clicks turned into signups or sales. Patterns appear fast. A certain intro hook, a specific kind of offer, or a particular spot in the runtime where you mention the link will keep showing up among your winners.

Treat those winners as a template. Repeat the format, the placement, and the phrasing that already converts, and stop reinventing the call to action on every upload. Evergreen videos are the real prize here, because a tutorial that keeps getting found will keep sending tracked clicks for months. Over a year, a handful of well placed description links on your best performers will out earn a far larger pile of videos that point nowhere measurable.

Frequently asked questions

How many links should I put in a YouTube description?

Keep one primary link above the fold. You can add secondary links lower down, but the first thing a viewer sees should be a single clear destination so the call to action does not compete with itself.

Do description links help with SEO or ranking?

Description links do not directly change how a video ranks. Their job is to convert attention into clicks you own and can measure, which is what turns views into traffic, signups, and revenue.

Where do I put a link on a YouTube Short?

Shorts hide most of the description, so use a pinned comment. Post your tracked link as the first comment and pin it, then reference it in the video so viewers know to look for it.

How do I know which video sent a click?

Use a unique tracked link or campaign tag per video. Every click is recorded with its source and campaign, so each visit ties back to the exact video that produced it.
